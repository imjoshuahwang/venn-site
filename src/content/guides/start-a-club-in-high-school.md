Starting a club is one of the fastest ways to turn an interest into something real at your school. The approval part is usually simple. The hard part is the fourth meeting, when the free pizza is gone and only the people who care keep showing up. This guide covers both: how to get your club approved, and how to build one that lasts.

## Step 1: Pick an idea with a reason to exist

Start with something you would do even if no one else joined. Then ask two questions:

- **Does a similar club already exist?** Check your school's club list, the student handbook, or ask the activities office. If one exists, consider joining and pitching a new project inside it, or make your club clearly different (a robotics club is not the same as a competitive drone racing team).
- **What will members actually do?** "A club for people who like business" is vague. "We build and run one real student business each semester" gives people a reason to come.

The best clubs are built around a recurring activity: building, competing, teaching, performing, publishing, or serving. Talking about a topic is not enough to keep people engaged.

## Step 2: Read your school's rules

Every school handles clubs differently. Find out:

- Who approves new clubs (activities director, student government, principal)
- Whether you need a minimum number of members or signatures
- Whether you need a written constitution
- Application deadlines (some schools only approve clubs at certain times of year)
- Rules about fundraising, money, and off-campus events

Ask the activities office directly. A five-minute conversation saves you from filling out the wrong form.

## Step 3: Find a faculty advisor

Almost every school requires a teacher or staff advisor. Your advisor signs forms, supervises meetings, and helps when something goes wrong.

Pick someone who connects to your topic and who you already have a good relationship with. When you ask, make it easy:

- Explain the club in two sentences
- Say how often you plan to meet and for how long
- Be clear about what you need from them (usually: be present, sign forms, offer advice) and what you will handle yourself (planning, communication, setup)

Teachers get asked a lot. If they say no, ask who else might be a fit.

## Step 4: Write a mission and a simple constitution

Your mission should be one or two sentences: what the club does and who it is for.

If your school requires a constitution, keep it short. Most cover:

1. Club name and purpose
2. Membership: who can join and what counts as active
3. Officer roles and how they are chosen
4. Meeting frequency
5. How decisions are made
6. How money is handled, if at all
7. How the constitution can be changed

Write down how leadership gets passed on. This is the part most founders skip, and it is the reason most clubs die when the founder graduates.

## Step 5: Gather founding members

Before you submit your application, find three to five people who are committed. Not "sure, sign me up" people. People who will help you plan the first meeting.

Then collect signatures or an interest list if your school requires one. Ask in classes related to your topic, post in group chats, and talk to people one on one. Personal asks work far better than flyers.

## Step 6: Submit and get approved

Fill out your school's form, attach your constitution and advisor signature, and turn it in before the deadline. Follow up if you do not hear back in a week or two. Once you are approved, ask about booking a room, getting listed in announcements, and appearing at any club fair.

## Step 7: Plan a first meeting that delivers

Your first meeting sets expectations. Do not spend it reading the constitution out loud.

- **Open with the point.** In two minutes, explain what the club will do this semester.
- **Do something.** Solve a problem, build a prototype, play a round, start a project. People should leave having done the thing the club is about.
- **Get contact info.** Use whatever your school allows: a group chat, an email list, or a class page.
- **Announce the next meeting** before anyone leaves.

## Step 8: Keep people coming back

This is where clubs succeed or fail.

### Give people a goal

A competition, a showcase, an event, a product launch, a fundraiser. A shared deadline creates momentum. Without one, attendance drifts down every week.

### Give people roles

Members who have a job (running social media, leading a project team, booking speakers) come back. Members who just watch tend to stop coming. Hand out responsibility early.

### Bring in outside people

Guest speakers, college students, and professionals in your field make meetings feel bigger than school. Email local college clubs and ask if a member would run a virtual session. Many are happy to. This is also what Venn does at scale: it connects high schoolers who are building things with college clubs and companies in their field.

### Track what you do

Keep a simple log: meeting dates, attendance, events, money raised, people served. You will need this for college applications, award nominations, and handing the club off.

## Step 9: Plan the handoff

By junior or senior year, start training the next leaders. Let them run meetings while you are still there to help. Write down passwords, contacts, and how-tos. A club that survives you is a much stronger story than one that ends when you leave.

## Club ideas that tend to work

If you need a starting point, these formats have a built-in activity:

- **Build clubs:** app development, robotics, hardware, game design, film production
- **Competition clubs:** debate, Model UN, math, science olympiad, case competitions, hackathons
- **Service clubs:** tutoring younger students, community cleanups, translation help for families
- **Publishing clubs:** student magazine, podcast, research journal, newsletter
- **Startup-style clubs:** members pitch and run small ventures each semester

Pick one where you can name the first three things members will do.

## FAQ

### How many members do you need to start a club in high school?

It depends on your school. Some schools require a minimum number of members or signatures, and others only need an advisor and a few interested students. Ask your activities office for the exact number.

### Can a freshman start a club?

At most schools, yes. Freshmen often have an advantage: you have four years to grow the club and train successors. Check whether your school has any grade restrictions on officer roles.

### What if my school says no to my club?

Ask why and what would change their decision. Common fixes are finding a different advisor, narrowing the focus, or showing more student interest. If the school still says no, you can run the project outside of school as an independent group, with a parent or mentor supervising.

### Does starting a club look good for college?

It can, especially if the club does something specific and you can show results: events held, members grown, competitions entered, people helped. Founding a club that met twice is less meaningful than steadily building one.

### How do you make a club successful?

Give it a concrete recurring activity, a goal with a deadline, and real roles for members. Then train the next leaders before you graduate.
