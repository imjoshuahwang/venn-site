You can start a nonprofit in high school. Plenty of students do. The catch is that "nonprofit" can mean two very different things: a project that does charitable work, or a legal entity recognized by the IRS as tax-exempt. You almost certainly want the first one right away and the second one later, if ever. This guide walks you through both, in the order that actually makes sense.

## Step 1: Start with the problem, not the paperwork

Most student nonprofits stall because the founder spends three months on a logo, a website, and a name, then has nothing to show anyone. Flip that order.

Pick one problem you care about and can reach directly. "Education inequality" is too big. "Middle schoolers at the school down the street have no free math tutoring" is something you can work on this month.

Then write a one-sentence mission: who you help, how, and where. For example: "We run free weekly coding workshops for 5th to 8th graders at the Eastside Public Library."

Before you build anything, check whether someone is already doing it. If a local organization runs the same program, you may get more done by starting a chapter with them or partnering than by competing for the same donors and volunteers.

## Step 2: Run a small pilot

Do the work once before you formalize anything. Host one workshop, run one drive, tutor five kids for a month. A pilot tells you:

- Whether people actually want what you are offering
- What it costs in money and hours
- Who is willing to help you consistently

It also gives you something real to point to when you ask a teacher, a donor, or a local business for support. "We tutored 18 students this fall and their parents want more sessions" beats any pitch deck.

## Step 3: Choose your structure

This is the decision that matters most. You have three realistic options.

### Option A: Run it as a school club or informal project

If your nonprofit mostly needs volunteers, a meeting space, and small amounts of money, a school club may be all you need. The school gives you an advisor, a room, and often a way to handle funds. Our guide on [how to start a club in high school](/guides/start-a-club-in-high-school/) covers that process.

The limit: donations to an informal project are not tax-deductible, and you may not be able to open a bank account in the project's name.

### Option B: Use a fiscal sponsor

A fiscal sponsor is an existing 501(c)(3) nonprofit that takes your project under its legal umbrella. Donors give to the sponsor, the sponsor holds the money for your project, and you spend it on your mission under the sponsor's rules. Donations can be tax-deductible because the sponsor is already tax-exempt.

For most high school students, this is the best route. You skip incorporation and the IRS application, you get financial oversight that makes donors trust you, and you can start raising money within weeks. Sponsors usually charge a fee, often a percentage of what you raise, so compare a few. Some sponsors focus specifically on student-led projects; Hack Club's HCB is one example used by many teen organizers. Read any sponsor's agreement with a parent before you sign.

### Option C: Incorporate and apply for 501(c)(3) status

This makes your organization its own legal entity. It is the most work and the most ongoing responsibility, and it is where being under 18 starts to matter (see the next section). It usually makes sense only after you have run for a while, have consistent funding, and have adults willing to serve on a board.

## Step 4: Understand the rules for minors

A few legal realities to plan around:

- **Incorporating.** Nonprofits are incorporated under state law, and states differ. Some states require incorporators to be at least 18. If yours does, a parent or another trusted adult can file as the incorporator while you lead the work.
- **Board of directors.** Most states let you have a board, and many have no minimum age for directors, but some do. New York, for example, requires directors to be at least 18. Check your state's nonprofit corporation law or ask your secretary of state's office. Even where minors can serve, it is smart to have adults on the board, since banks, donors, and grant makers often expect it.
- **Contracts and bank accounts.** Contracts signed by minors can often be canceled by the minor, which makes banks and vendors cautious. Expect to need an adult officer to open and co-sign the organization's bank account.
- **EIN.** Your organization needs an Employer Identification Number from the IRS. The [online EIN application](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/get-an-employer-identification-number) requires a "responsible party," an individual with an SSN or ITIN who controls the organization. Many student founders have an adult officer serve as the responsible party.

None of this means you cannot lead. It means you build a small team of adults who sign things while you run the mission.

## Step 5: If you incorporate, follow this order

If you go with Option C, here is the general sequence:

1. **Choose a name** and check that it is available with your state's business filing office.
2. **Recruit a board.** Most states require a minimum number of directors. Pick people who will show up: a teacher, a parent, a local professional in your field.
3. **File articles of incorporation** with your state. The articles need specific language about charitable purpose and what happens to assets if you dissolve to qualify for 501(c)(3) status. The IRS publishes [sample language](https://www.irs.gov/charities-non-profits/suggested-language-for-corporations-and-associations) you can adapt.
4. **Adopt bylaws and a conflict-of-interest policy** at your first board meeting, and keep minutes.
5. **Get an EIN** from the IRS.
6. **Apply for tax-exempt status.** You file Form 1023 or the shorter Form 1023-EZ online through Pay.gov. The IRS currently lists the [user fee](https://www.irs.gov/charities-non-profits/form-1023-and-1023-ez-amount-of-user-fee) as $600 for Form 1023 and $275 for Form 1023-EZ, so confirm before filing.
7. **Register with your state** if required. Many states have their own steps for charities, such as registering before you solicit donations or applying separately for state tax exemptions.

### Can you use Form 1023-EZ?

Probably, if you are small. Per the [Form 1023-EZ instructions](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i1023ez), you generally qualify if your annual gross receipts have not exceeded $50,000 in any of the past three years and are not projected to exceed $50,000 in the next three, and your total assets are $250,000 or less. You must complete the eligibility worksheet in the instructions before filing. Some types of organizations cannot use it at all, so read the worksheet carefully.

## Step 6: Stay compliant after you launch

Getting 501(c)(3) status is not the finish line. Most exempt organizations must file an annual information return with the IRS. Small organizations whose gross receipts are normally $50,000 or less can usually file the short [Form 990-N (e-Postcard)](https://www.irs.gov/charities-non-profits/annual-electronic-filing-requirement-for-small-exempt-organizations-form-990-n-e-postcard). If you fail to file for three consecutive years, the IRS automatically revokes your exemption.

Also plan for what happens when you graduate. Write down who keeps the board running, who has the bank login, and who files the annual return. A nonprofit that disappears when its founder leaves for college does not help anyone.

## Step 7: Raise money and grow

Start small and local:

- **Ask local businesses** for in-kind donations (supplies, space, printing) before asking for cash.
- **Run one well-organized fundraiser** with a specific goal: "$800 buys calculators for 40 students."
- **Apply for youth grants** from community foundations, Rotary and Lions clubs, and companies with local giving programs.
- **Track everything** in a simple spreadsheet: every dollar in, every dollar out, every person served. Donors and grant makers will ask.

Growth usually comes from partners, not marketing. College clubs and organizations in your field can share volunteers, expertise, and audiences. ## Common mistakes to avoid

- Incorporating before you have a working program
- Collecting donations into your personal Venmo with no records
- Promising donors their gift is tax-deductible when it is not
- Building a board of friends who will not show up
- Going it alone when a fiscal sponsor or existing nonprofit could carry the legal weight

## FAQ

### Can a 16-year-old start a nonprofit?

Yes. You can start and lead a charitable project at any age. Formally incorporating may require an adult incorporator or adult directors depending on your state, and you will likely need an adult to co-sign bank accounts. A fiscal sponsor lets you operate without incorporating at all.

### How much does it cost to start a nonprofit?

A fiscally sponsored project can cost little upfront beyond the sponsor's fee on money you raise. Incorporating costs your state's filing fee plus the IRS user fee, currently listed as $275 for Form 1023-EZ or $600 for Form 1023. State fees vary.

### Do I need 501(c)(3) status to start a nonprofit?

No. You need it for your own organization to receive tax-deductible donations and qualify for many grants. A fiscal sponsor can give your project access to those benefits through its existing status.

### Does starting a nonprofit help with college applications?

It can, if it is real. What stands out is the impact you can document and what you learned, not the legal status. A school club that tutored 200 kids is more impressive than a 501(c)(3) that exists only on paper.

### How long does it take to get 501(c)(3) status?

It varies with the form and the IRS's current workload. Form 1023-EZ applications are generally processed faster than the full Form 1023. Check IRS.gov for current processing times.
